P0012 [Heading 1] Introduction
P0013 [normal] The integration of automated surveillance systems in smart city environments is crucial for public safety. However, traditional threat detection frameworks often rely on unimodal visual data, for example, standard RGB CCTV footage. These single modality systems have many limitations. Although CCTV cameras are capable of accurately detecting open-carry weapons, they are incapable of detecting concealed weapons, which makes achieving preemptive security more difficult. Alternative sensor systems, such as Passive Millimeter Wave (PMMW), thermal, or 77GHz imaging, can, however, successfully penetrate materials (e.g., clothing) to identify concealed objects like knives. Yet relying only on weapon detection often generates high false-positive rates, as there is not enough context for the system to accurately determine malicious intent.
P0014 [normal] Behavioural analysis through Facial Expression Recognition (FER) provides deep insight into an individual’s psychological state and intent. Yet, using only FER to predict threats is flawed as well since an individual exhibiting aggressive or fearful emotional state is not necessarily an armed physical threat. These limitations are naturally overcome by human security operators as they fuse multiple types of information such as a person’s physical state and their apparent intent in order to determine the existence of a threat. However, humans are prone to vigilance decrement with recent studies confirming that within the first 15-20 minutes of sustained monitoring, detection rates plummet (Warm, Parasuraman and Matthews, 2008). Consequently, modern security solutions must shift away from manual monitoring towards multi-modal automated threat prediction systems.
P0015 [normal] This project proposes a multi-modal machine learning framework that mimics this human cognitive assessment by fusing data from Concealed/Open Carry Weapon Detection (CWD) and Facial Expression Recognition (FER). By integrating the outputs of these two distinct modalities, the system aims to efficiently calculate a unified, real-time threat probability score, significantly reducing false positives compared to single modality baselines.
P0017 [normal] A primary challenge in this domain is the computational complexity of multi-modal architectures, which often prevents them from being deployed on resource constrained edge devices. To ensure practical usage in real-world smart city environments, this project focuses on edge computing by utilizing optimized and lightweight models. The proposed framework is designed to operate on edge devices with low latency. Furthermore, the core research of this project will address the significant gap in multi-modal literature regarding dataset availability. Because natively paired FER and CWD datasets do not exist, this project will investigate fusion methodologies to effectively integrate asynchronous sensor data. Ultimately, this project aims to deliver a lightweight, privacy-conscious, and accurate threat prediction system suitable for edge deployment.

P0082 [Heading 1] Literature Review
P0083 [normal] Introduction
P0084 [normal] Research on automation threat detection has grown in recent years, especially in areas such as public safety, surveillance, cybersecurity, and smart city systems. Much of this work has focused on detecting danger from visual data, such as identifying concealed/open carry weapons, recognising suspicious behaviour, or estimating a person’s emotional state from their facial expression. These approaches can be useful, but each one has limitations when used on its own. A weapon may be present without any real malicious intent, while an angry or upset individual does not necessarily mean that they are dangerous. Because of this, unimodal frameworks often struggle to make reliable threat predictions and may trigger many false alarms.
P0085 [normal] A lot of the literature also shows that system performance can drop when they move away from controlled laboratory environments and into more real world, “in-the-wild” settings. This performance drop is mainly due to extreme changes in lighting, camera angles, image quality, and background activity which can make detection more difficult. This is why more recent research has moved towards systems that can work with multiple types of information at once, for example, combining emotion, body movement, crowd dynamics, sound, or even textual data such as logs and emails. These multi modal systems are often more reliable because they can use one source of information to support or correct another.
P0086 [normal] For this project, the most relevant areas of research are concealed/open carry weapon detection (CWD), facial expression recognition (FER) in the wild, lightweight detection models for edge devices, and methods for combining separate model outputs into one unified decision. The literature in these areas helps show what has already been achieved as well as address the main gaps that are present. In particular, there is still limited work on combining CWD with FER for real time threat prediction. This literature review therefore focuses on the methods, strengths, and weaknesses of current work in order to justify proposed system’s design choices and show how it could derive useful contributions.
P0087 [normal] Concealed/Open Carry Weapon Detection (CWD)
P0088 [normal] Overview
P0089 [normal] Concealed weapon detection aims to identify dangerous objects such as guns or knives that are hidden on the human body. This is a difficult problem because the object may be partially or fully covered by clothing, bags, body position, lighting, or poor image quality. The literature shows that different sensing methods have been used for this task, including RGB images, thermal imaging, passive millimetre wave imaging (PMMW), and radar systems. However, there is no single “best” sensing method. While RGB data is more accessible, it cannot detect concealed objects. Specialised sensing methods can improve detection but often require more complex hardware and rarer datasets. Because of this, research in this area has gradually moved from simple image processing methods towards learned models that can better handle variation in real scenes.
P0090 [normal] Early methods
P0091 [normal] Some earlier work relied on classical image processing methods such as colour based segmentation, Harris corner detection, Active Appearance Models, Local Binary Patterns, SIFT, and SURF to locate shape or patterns in the image indicative of the presence of a weapon. While in simpler and more controlled settings these approaches could produce acceptable results, they were often sensitive to noise, background activity, and changes in viewing conditions. As a result, these methods do not generalise well to real life conditions and therefore more recent studies have moved towards deep learning methods (Mahajan and Padha, 2018; Sandhu and Bhatia, 2025; Warsi et al., 2020). 
P0092 [normal] Evolution of recent CWD methods
P0093 [normal] R-CNN - One of the earliest deep learning frameworks, which worked by initially generating a set of candidate regions in the image where an object might be and then passing each candidate region through a convolutional neural network (CNN) to extract features before classifying the object. This approach improved detection accuracy compared with many earlier methods because it allowed the model to learn useful patterns directly from data. However, R-CNN was also slow, because each region had to be processed separately, making it too slow for real time tasks (Sandhu and Bhatia, 2025).
P0094 [normal] Fast R-CNN - To address R-CNN’s main weakness, Fast R-CNN introduced a more efficient design. Instead of running the CNN on each candidate region separately, it processed the full image once to produce a feature map, and then extracted region specific features from that map. Although this made the computation faster, it still depended on the slow, external step mentioned above to generate the candidate regions (Sandhu and Bhatia, 2025).
P0095 [normal] Faster R-CNN - An even more efficient version which eliminated the primary speed bottleneck by adding a candidate region network inside the model so the regions are learned rather than generated separately. This improvement made the model much more efficient and accurate, yet still usually too slow for adequate real time detection (Sandhu and Bhatia, 2025).
P0096 [normal] YOLO (which stands for You Only Look Once) - Became popular because it is a one stage method. Instead of generating candidate regions first, it predicts object classes and bounding boxes directly from the image in one scan. This speed advantage makes the model highly practical and suitable for real time usage. There are smaller versions of YOLO designed for edge devices and real time deployment by reducing model size and computation, which in turn also reduce detection accuracy due to the mentioned trade-off. This lightweight variant of YOLO is likely the most relevant direction for this project (Sandhu and Bhatia, 2025).
P0097 [normal] Non-visual sensing methods
P0098 [normal] Thermal imaging - Thermal imaging works by detecting the infrared radiation emitted by objects. In the context of weapon detection, it is useful because a human body naturally emits heat, whereas a cold object like a metal knife or gun does not. However, thermal detection relies entirely on secondary heat transfer. A knife is only revealed if it alters the surface temperature of thin, form fitting clothing, creating a silhouette on the thermal image. An important advantage for thermal cameras is that they emit no radiation and are safe for public use. However, they struggle to penetrate thick clothing and remain unreliable for real world CWD. Muñoz et al. (2025) show that this approach is possible, however, the implementation is too slow for practical real time deployment. Their results are mAP@50-95 = 64.52% with a frame rate of 2 FPS.
P0099 [normal] Passive Millimetre-Wave (PMMW) imaging - This kind of passive and harmless imaging captures the natural electromagnetic radiation emitted by the human body. PMMW imaging operates in a frequency range (30 - 300 GHz) such that these longer wavelengths easily penetrate clothing, causing a concealed knife to create a dark silhouette against the bright thermal background of the body. This advantage makes PMMW highly effective for CWD tasks. This approach is a highly relevant direction for this project.
Pang et al. (2020) confirms the project's design choice to utilise YOLO based models with PMMW imaging as they attempted to detect metallic weapons concealed under clothing in real time, with their results as the following on a SAIR-U dataset with 1634 PMMW images.
P0102 [normal] Radar imaging - this method classifies as an active imaging method as it emits low power radio signals (e.g., 77 GHz) and measures the energy reflected back from the target. Radar waves pass through clothing and bounce off solid objects. This method provides depth perception and 3D mapping capabilities which comes at the cost of more specialized hardware requirements as well as privacy considerations.
P0103 [normal] Gao et al. (2022) proposed a 77 GHz radar based system which performs well in real time, however, the sensor and target must be in close proximity (less than 6 meters) and the system is limited to a single carried object.
P0104 [normal] Radar based walk through body scanners can achieve accurate real time CWD by reconstructing 3D voxel images and applying 3D CNNs, but they require specialised multi panel radar hardware and infrastructure not suitable for lightweight deployments (Khan et al., n.d.).
P0105 [normal] Performance and trade-offs in modern CWD
P0106 [normal] The reason R-CNN based methods still cannot operate in real time speed is because they are two-stage methods with the first stage being the candidate regions.
P0107 [normal] In general, much of the literature confirms that higher detection accuracy usually comes at the cost of speed. The speed limitation that R-CNN based models presented led to the development of YOLO based methods.
P0108 [normal] In the context of surveillance, a model must simultaneously maintain a high frame rate to handle real time data without latency, as well as achieve a high Mean Average Precision (mAP) to minimise false negatives.
mAP is the standard evaluation metric in object detection which combines classification accuracy and bounding-box precision into a unified [0-1] score. 
P0109 [normal] Sandhu et al. (2025) conclude in their comprehensive survey that YOLO based models offer the best real time performance (balancing speed and accuracy) across various deep learning models. However, they also note that most existing CWD systems are not yet optimized for low-cost edge deployment and many systems that were trained on high quality datasets performed poorly on low resolution, noisy CCTV data, leading to a high rate of false alarms. This highlights the ongoing need for lightweight, robust CWD solutions that perform well in real world environments (Sandhu and Bhatia, 2025).
P0110 [normal] Facial Expression Recognition(FER)
P0111 [normal] Overview
P0112 [normal] Facial Expression Recognition aims to automatically identify the emotional state of a person by analysing their facial features. This process typically follows a standard pipeline. Initially the face is detected in an image, then the facial landmarks (which are precise points used to analyse and track key facial regions like the lips and eyes) are aligned, the visual features are extracted, and finally the expression is classified into emotion categories such as anger, fear, happiness, or neutral (Pise et al., 2022). While FER has seen drastic progress, it remains a highly challenging problem when deployed outside of controller laboratory settings. Real world, “in-the-wild” data introduces extreme variations in lighting, head pose, facial occlusion (e.g., masks or glasses), and image quality. Because this project aims to predict threats in real world settings, understanding how FER models maintain accuracy under these difficult conditions is crucial and will be a primary focus for this project.
P0113 [normal] Early FER methods
P0114 [normal] Early approaches to FER relied heavily on traditional machine learning and hand crafted feature extraction. These methods typically extracted geometric features of appearance features from the face. Once these features were extracted, standard classifiers such as SVM, MLP, or Random Forests were used to predict the emotion (Pise et al., 2022).
P0115 [normal] During this period, models were mostly trained and tested on laboratory controlled datasets like CK+, MMI, and JAFFE. These datasets had very forgiving conditions with the subjects facing directly at the camera, with perfect lighting, and exaggerated expressions. While early methods achieved high accuracy on these datasets, the literature shows that they suffered from severe overfitting and their performance dropped when applied to natural expressions in real world settings. This limitation led to the development of deep learning FER models as well as the focus on larger, “in-the-wild” datasets (Li and Deng, 2020; Pise et al., 2022).
P0116 [normal] CNN-Based FER
P0117 [normal] Instead of relying on hand crafted features, CNNs learn visual features directly from the raw image. This ability made them much more effective at capturing subtle changes in facial muscles and local texture patterns, which are essential for differentiating between similar looking expressions such as fear, anger, and surprise. As a result, CNN based methods quickly became the dominant approach for FER tasks, especially when larger image datasets became available (Li and Deng, 2020).
P0118 [normal] A standard CNN based FER process usually begins with face detection and alignment, followed by image normalisation and resizing before the face is passed through the network for classification [li and deng]. The network then learns abstract facial representations across the layers which allows it to adapt better to variation in the data. However, while CNNs improved performance, the literature shows that they still depend heavily on good dataset quality and often suffer from overfitting when trained on small datasets (Li and Deng, 2020).
P0119 [normal] ResNet based FER - A common choice in FER because it uses skip connections to help information flow through deeper networks which makes training more stable. For FER tasks, this deeper feature extraction technique is useful since emotion differences can be very subtle. The main drawback is that similarly to CWD, stronger performance often comes with higher computational cost (He et al., 2016).
P0120 [normal] MobileNet based FER - This lightweight variant is designed for efficiency and thus a very likely direction for the project. It uses depthwise separable convolutions, which split the normal convolution operation into smaller steps and greatly reduce the number of calculations. Although MobileNet is much faster and lighter than other CNN based methods, it is still accurate enough for practical use (Liang et al., 2023).
P0121 [normal] Xception based FER - Similar to MobileNet but it applies the depthwise separable convolutions more aggressively. Xception balances accuracy and computational cost which is why it appears often in literature. Its main limitation is that it is still heavier than other lightweight models (Chollet, 2016).
P0122 [normal] Attention based FER - Recently, more CNN based FER models add attention mechanisms which help the network focus on the most informative facial regions, such as the eyes, eyebrows, and mouth. This technique is useful since not every part of the face contributes equally to every emotion. Adding attention mechanisms improves robustness at the cost of increased model complexity, therefore usage in edge deployment must be cautious (Sun, Zhao and Jin, 2018; Chakrapani Ghadai, Patra and Manish Okade, 2024).
P0123 [normal] Benchmarking studies and model comparison
P0124 [normal] The strength of CNN based FER is further supported by large benchmarking work. Tutuianu et al. (2024) evaluated 23 CNN based methods, including VGG, ResNet, DenseNet, MobileNet, EfficientNet, and Inception. These models were tested across the popular, “in-the-wild” RAF-DB, FER2013, AffectNet datasets as well as their own highly balanced BTFER testing set for cross dataset validation. Their results showed that standard CNN architectures such as ResNet, DenseNet, MobileNet, and EfficientNet consistently ranked among the strongest performers when configured with pretraining and balanced training procedures. A key finding is that many systems perform less reliably when evaluated on unfamiliar data which is likely due to the large class imbalance issue present with many of the standard datasets (many more examples of “happy” than “anger”) (Testa et al., 2019). Therefore, the inclusion of BTFER is useful since it provides a highly balanced, diverse, real world test set which may give a more credible evaluation to an FER model. Another important finding is that performance depends on more than just the CNN architecture. Pretraining on ImageNet or VGGFace consistently improved results compared to training from scratch. The study also found that mid range resolutions (around 224x224) often performed better than low resolution inputs such as those used in FER2013.

P0127 [normal] Lightweight and edge friendly FER
P0128 [normal] Benchmarking studies also show that not all CNN architectures are suitable for deployment. Heavy models such as some ResNet, DenseNet, and EfficientNet variants require higher memory and computational costs, albeit being more accurate. Therefore, since the project aims for real time use on 
P0129 [normal] resource constrained decides, then the FER component must also be lightweight.
P0130 [normal] One important direction in this area is the design of compact FER architectures from the start. Liang et al. (2023) proposed an improved MobileNetV3 model. Although this model is already lightweight and is designed for mobile use, they have further reduced computational cost by shrinking the inverted residual bottlenecks and the second to last fully connected layer. To mitigate the reduced capacity, the improved model adds a coordinate attention mechanism (Hou, Zhou and Feng, 2021) and a pooling structure so that it can focus more strongly on informative facial regions while still preserving global context. The reported results are relevant to this project as the model achieved 87.5% accuracy on FERPlus (which is an improved version of FER2013) and 86.6% on RAF-DB, while having low hardware requirements.
P0131 [normal] A second direction is model compression for edge deployment. Pascual et al. proposed Light-FER, which compresses an Xception based FER model using pruning, quantisation, and hardware specific optimisation. Pruning removes less important weights, quantisation reduces the numerical precision of the model to lower memory, and compilers such as TensorRT can then optimise the model for specific edge hardware. Their results show that this direction works. The compressed model retained accuracy close to the original while reaching around 27 FPS on a local PC and around 6 FPS on an NVIDIA Jetson Nano. 
P0132 [normal] Out of the 2 mentioned strategies, the first appears more relevant for this project because the FER component will need to operate alongside the CWD one, meaning the total system load will be higher than in a standalone FER system. The literature therefore supports the use of lightweight MobileNet based architectures as a more practical starting point, while compression methods remain valuable as a secondary optimisation step if needed.
P0133 [normal] FER in challenging conditions
P0134 [normal] As the literature has established [X], models that perform well on lab-controlled datasets often deteriorate when the face is partially covered, the lighting is poor, or the camera angle is awkward. This finding is relevant for this project because real life surveillance footage is likely to have the complications above and the model will need to handle noisy and incomplete inputs if the aim is to be useful in a real setting.
P0135 [normal] Mukhiddinov et al. (2023) is a good example of how researchers have tried to address the notorious partial occlusion problem, which in this case, relates to performing FER on a masked face. Their method focuses on the upper face by using facial landmarks and low light enhancement before passing the input into the model. It is important to note that partial occlusion is extremely challenging to address because it removes many of the cues that ordinary FER systems rely on. They report 69.3% accuracy on a synthetically masked version of AffectNet, which shows that FER can still work under difficult conditions, although the performance is likely to drop.
P0136 [normal] In real surveillance settings, the masked face problem extends to blur, low lighting, motion, and partial occlusion from glasses or hats. The literature confirms that the model must learn to focus on more stable facial regions or be trained on data that reflects these complications. This is one reason why “in-the-wild” datasets are so important when training or evaluating FER systems as well as why cross dataset validation gives much more credible results (Li and Deng, 2018; Pise et al., 2022).
P0137 [normal] Multi Modal Fusion Strategies in Threat Prediction
P0138 [normal] Overview
P0139 [normal] Multi modal threat prediction combines different sources of information, such as facial expression, object detection, pose, audio, crowd dynamics, text, or even cybersecurity logs to produce a more reliable assessment of risk than any single modality alone. In surveillance environments, this is important because a system based on only one input may miss key warning cues. Recent studies show that multi modal fusion can make threat prediction more context aware (S, Shafana and Easter, 2026). For this project, that idea is relevant because concealed weapon detection and facial expression recognition capture different aspects of possible danger, therefore the main challenge is deciding how to combine them into one meaningful threat estimate in such a way that the separate modalities complement or support each other.
P0140 [normal] Feature level
P0141 [normal] A common strategy, in which raw inputs or low level features from different modalities are combined before the main classification stage. This approach usually means concatenating feature vectors early in the pipeline so that a single model can learn joint representations from the beginning. 
P0142 [normal] A major advantage of feature level fusion is that it gives the model access to richer combined evidence, which can improve performance when the modalities are strongly related. This approach is appealing in controlled multi modal environments where the inputs come from the same event.
P0143 [normal] However, an important limitation is that it assumes that the modalities can be aligned accurately before fusion, which is not always realistic in surveillance based systems.
P0144 [normal] Intermediate fusion
P0145 [normal] Intermediate fusion combines modalities after each one has first been processed into a learned feature representation, but before the final prediction is made. It sits between feature level fusion and decision level fusion, and is often used when the modalities are related enough to benefit from a shared representation but still distinct enough to need separate encoders. Examples of intermediate fusion implementations are through concatenation, attention, gating, or cross-attention, which allow the model to preserve modality specific information while also learning interactions between them.
P0146 [normal] Several recent studies use this style of fusion in threat related multi modal systems. Hussain et al. (2026) combine surveillance video, text, and cybersecurity logs using intermediate fusion through cross-modal attention and gating. Raja et al. (2025) adopts a conditional attention mechanism over video, audio, and environmental inputs so that more informative modalities receive greater weight during anomaly detection. Ahmad, Khan and Sajjad (2025) use gated fusion networks for violence detection, where extracted modality features are merged in a shared space and a gating module balances the influence of RGB, optical flow, and audio streams. Similarly, N et al. (2025) applies cross-attention to fuse video, audio, and network telemetry for smart-city threat detection, demonstrating how intermediate fusion can support real-time reasoning across different data types.
P0147 [normal] Although these studies show that intermediate fusion can be effective, it is not the most relevant direction for this project as FER and CWD are not naturally aligned at the sample level.
P0148 [normal] Decision level
P0149 [normal] Overall, the literature suggests that fusion becomes more difficult as modalities become less aligned and more heterogeneous. Methods that work well for tightly related streams, such as video-audio inputs, are less suitable when the modalities are independently collected and only loosely related at the decision stage.
P0150 [normal] Decision level fusion is one of the most common fusion strategies, where outputs from the separate modalities are combined into a final risk judgement. Examples of decision level fusion methods include weighted decision methods, classical inference, Bayesian inference, and Dempster–Shafer method. This strategy is useful in surveillance settings in particular as it is easier to interpret, debug, and more practical when each modality is developed as a separate component.
P0151 [normal] Chatzis, Bors and Pitas (1999) proposed a multi modal person authentication framework that utilises decision level fusion by taking the outputs of separate unimodal classifiers and combining them using fuzzy clustering methods and a median radial basis function network. 
P0152 [normal] S, Shafana and Easter (2026) developed VisionSentinel, which combines emotion, pose, and crowd dynamics in a hybrid fusion engine which integrates rule based logic for critical situations with a Random Forest classifier otherwise.
P0153 [normal] For the present project, this type of fusion is highly relevant because both CWD and FER components will be developed separately and can be treated as so before being combined into a final threat score. A decision level approach would support a more interpretable justification for why a person is classified as higher risk.
P0154 [normal] Identifying the Research Gap and Conclusion
P0155 [normal] The literature reviewed across concealed/open carry weapon detection, facial expression recognition, and multimodal threat analysis shows that strong progress has been made in each area individually, but important limitations remain when these methods are considered as a single system.
In concealed weapon detection, modern object detectors, particularly YOLO based architectures, are widely recognised for offering the best balance between speed and detection accuracy for surveillance applications, making them attractive for real time deployment.
Similarly, in facial expression recognition, lightweight CNN architectures such as MobileNet variants have been shown to provide a practical trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency, especially when compared with heavier models that are less suitable for resource-constrained hardware. These findings suggest that both YOLO and MobileNet are strong unimodal candidates for edge-based detection tasks.
P0156 [normal] However, the literature also heavily confirms that unimodal success does not translate into an effective end to end threat prediction system. Many of the mentioned studies have shown that combining different modalities can improve context awareness and performance.
P0157 [normal] The main research gap is not the absence of multimodal threat detection research, but the lack of a lightweight and interpretable framework that combines concealed weapon detection with facial expression recognition for real time threat prediction. 
P0158 [normal] The proposed project directly addresses this gap by developing separate lightweight CWD and FER components, then integrating their outputs through a decision level fusion strategy designed for asynchronous and diverse inputs.
